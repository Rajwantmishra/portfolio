01 / Leadership

How I lead

The hardest part of enterprise AI is rarely the model. It is deciding where to invest, designing the operating model around it, and getting people to use what gets built. That is where most of my time goes.

01

Choosing what to fund

Good AI investment starts with better questions.

  • –Is the data actually there?
  • –Who owns the process after launch?
  • –What will it cost to run, not build?
  • –Would buying be cheaper?

Saying no early, and saying why, is a large part of the job.

02

Turning a decision into a capability

A funded idea is not a capability yet.

  • –Architecture and team in place before the pilot
  • –Governance built into delivery, not bolted on after
  • –Shared platform instead of a bespoke build each time

One integration, not a project — teams stop waiting for permission.

03

Building the team

Distributed teams run on clear ownership, not process documents.

  • –16–30 people, 4 countries
  • –Whole problems handed over, business context included
  • –Leads grown internally, not hired around

Most decisions get made without me in the room — that only works if everyone knows what they own.

04

Measuring what matters

No AI program gets defended on enthusiasm.

  • –Cost removed, hours returned
  • –Accuracy in production, not in a demo
  • –How many people actually use it

Better to find out in month two than month twenty.

02 / Impact

From opportunity to outcome

Four decisions and what came of them. The business story is on the surface. The build is underneath, for anyone who wants it.

Translation and content formatting brought in-house

Cost structure
The challenge

Clinical and commercial content depended on third-party translation and formatting vendors. Cost scaled with volume, and turnaround set the pace of every launch.

My role

Made the case to build rather than renew, owned the budget and vendor exit, and designed and built the pipeline myself alongside the team.

Outcome

Multimillion-dollar annual savings

Vendors replaced in-house; turnaround now set by us, not a contract.

Why it matteredRecurring vendor spend became an owned capability, which changed what the organization believed it could build.

How it was built ↓

A proprietary NLP and LLM pipeline covering translation, terminology consistency, and layout-preserving formatting, run on internal infrastructure so clinical content never left our control.

Related published research: the Ice Cube algorithm, an adaptive time-stretching approach that keeps AI-translated multilingual video in sync with its source.

Document processing that never leaves the building

Applied ML
The challenge

High-volume document work was manual, and the documents in question could not be sent to an external model API.

My role

Chose the on-premises open-weight route over a hosted service, set the accuracy bar for production, and owned the build.

Outcome

~90% production accuracy

Annual savings around 10x the cost of running it.

Why it matteredIt settled an argument. Data residency is not a reason to skip automation, it is a design constraint.

How it was built ↓

An open-weight model fine-tuned on proprietary documents, hosted on premises, using a retrieval-augmented fine-tuning approach so the model was grounded in our own reference material rather than trained from scratch.

ButlerAI: adopting AI as one integration, not a project

Platform
The challenge

Every team that wanted AI was rebuilding the same plumbing: model access, a front end, onboarding, authentication, audit. Each use case became its own project.

My role

Argued for platform investment ahead of the demand, then set the architecture and the standard teams build against.

Outcome

One interface, whole stack inherited

Audit and governance centralized, no gate added in front of delivery.

Why it matteredIt changed the unit of AI adoption from a project to a decision, which is the difference between a handful of pilots and a portfolio.

How it was built ↓

An internal platform built around the Model Context Protocol. It abstracts LLM integration, interface, user onboarding, authentication, and audit behind one standard. A team exposes a single MCP server and inherits the rest of the stack automatically.

AI chat across regulated and unregulated domains, without mixing the data

Governance
The challenge

Internal teams and public-facing sites both needed conversational access to company knowledge, but medical content carries obligations that marketing content does not.

My role

Set the separation principle up front and carried it through compliance review rather than retrofitting controls after launch.

Outcome

Two fully separate infrastructures

One service pattern, deployed internally and across group-facing sites.

Why it matteredCompliance designed in at the start costs a fraction of compliance added after the fact, and it is the only version that scales.

How it was built ↓

A retrieval-augmented chat service with strictly separate medical and non-medical infrastructure, indexes, and access paths, so no retrieval step can cross the boundary.

Forensic incident processing

Security

Finding PHI and PII exposure inside breach-related logs and leaked datasets fast enough to scope the notification population. Built with a forensics partner, alongside compliance and legal.

BookMySpot

Automation

AI joined to the telephony stack and to legacy backends, handling booking, cancellations, and order status. Adopted across several business units without replacing the systems underneath.

05 / Journey

How I got here

I started in enterprise systems, spent years close to the code and the data, and moved into leadership because the interesting problems kept turning out to be organizational. A master's in data science along the way, to strengthen the analytical foundation behind the decisions.

2007 → 2016

Enterprise systems, consulting

Transformation work for Fortune 500 companies including Kraft Heinz, Colgate-Palmolive, and NuSkin, through Infosys, Deloitte, and Cognizant. Built trade promotion systems on SAP CRM with predictive modeling. Learned how large organizations really make decisions, which is not how the org chart suggests.

2016 → 2023

Digital and AI, in-house

Led digital transformation across sales, CRM, and marketing at a global eyewear and eyecare group. Owned AI integration across SAP CRM and Salesforce, putting real-time insight in front of more than 500 sales representatives. First taste of adoption being the hard part.

2023 → now

Data science and AI leadership

Accountable for AI and data science across the group's medical and clinical portfolio. A distributed organization of 16 to 30 people, a seven-figure budget, more than ten concurrent initiatives, and a multi-country rollout of AI clinical translation and scribe tooling.

What has stayed constant is the shape of the problem. Someone wants an outcome, the technology is only part of the answer, and the rest is judgment about people, sequencing, and cost. The AI era has made that gap wider, not narrower.

What I care about now is building teams where good engineers become good leaders, and making decisions that still look reasonable two years later. I write when I have something worth arguing, which is roughly once a month.
